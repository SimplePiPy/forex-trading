## Read this before you walk your yard

Most trees don't fall without warning. They give you months, sometimes years. The problem is that the warnings don't look like warnings. They look like mushrooms, or a little crack, or a few dead branches up top that you figure the tree will grow out of.

It won't.

Here are the five things I look at first on every property I walk. You can check all five in about ten minutes. You don't need a ladder and you should not climb anything.

---

## 1 · A lean that wasn't there before

Plenty of trees lean. A tree that grew crooked toward the light has been doing that for forty years and it's fine.

What matters is a **new** lean, or a lean that's getting worse.

**Walk to the base and look at the ground on the side opposite the lean.** If you see cracked soil, a lifted mat of turf, or exposed roots that look like the tree is pulling out of the ground, that's root plate failure. The tree is already coming down. It just hasn't finished.

**Call somebody today if:** the soil is heaving, or the lean changed after a storm.

---

## 2 · Mushrooms or hard shelves growing on the trunk, the base, or the roots

This is the one I get called about last and should get called about first.

Homeowners see mushrooms and think it's a wet year. What you're actually looking at is the fruiting body of a fungus that has been eating the inside of the tree for years. By the time it fruits on the outside, there is already significant decay on the inside.

**Look for:** soft mushrooms clustered at the base after rain, or hard woody shelves growing straight out of the trunk or a root flare. Anything shelf-shaped and firm is a bad sign.

A tree can look completely healthy up top and be hollow at the base. The leaves don't tell you what the trunk is doing.

**Call somebody if:** you see any shelf fungus on the trunk or root flare, full stop.

---

## 3 · A V-shaped split where two trunks meet

This is the one everybody misses, and it's the one that drops half a tree on a roof in a summer thunderstorm.

Look up at where the tree divides. A **U-shaped** union, wide and rounded, is strong. A **V-shaped** union, tight and narrow, where the two stems press against each other, is not. Bark gets trapped in that seam instead of wood growing across it, so there's nothing holding the two halves together except the pressure of them leaning on each other.

**Look for:** a tight V, a dark seam running down from the crotch, or a visible crack. Any bleeding, oozing, or a raised ridge along that seam means it's already moving.

These fail on a calm day with wet leaves. They fail on a good day.

**Call somebody if:** the union is tight and V-shaped and there's anything you care about underneath it.

---

## 4 · Dead branches at the top of the tree

Deadwood on the lower inside of a canopy is normal. Those branches got shaded out and died. That's just how trees work.

Dead branches **at the very top**, in the outer canopy where the most light is, mean something else. The tree can't get water and nutrients to its furthest point. That's usually a root problem, a vascular disease, or decay in the trunk.

Crown dieback that's spreading downward year over year is a tree in decline. The dead limbs themselves are also the immediate hazard, because dead wood comes out of a canopy in wind with no warning.

**Call somebody if:** the top is thinning or dying back and it's worse than it was last year.

---

## 5 · Anything that changed within 20 feet of the trunk in the last five years

This is the one nobody connects, because the damage happens years before the tree responds.

A tree's roots go out further than its branches and most of them sit in the top 18 inches of soil. So:

- A trench for a water line, gas line, or internet
- A new driveway, patio, or addition
- Regrading, or fill dirt piled over the root zone
- Heavy equipment parked on the root zone during a build
- A root cut on one side during landscaping

Any of those can kill a tree slowly. It'll hold on for three to seven years looking mostly fine, then decline fast. If you had work done and the tree has been thinning ever since, that's the reason.

**Call somebody if:** there's been construction, trenching, or grade change near a tree you want to keep.

---

## What to do with this

**If none of the five show up:** you're probably fine. Walk it again after the next big storm and once a year in the spring.

**If one shows up:** get eyes on it. Not urgent, but don't file it away and forget.

**If two or more show up on the same tree, or if it's leaning over a house, a driveway, or a place people sit:** get it looked at now. Trees that fail have usually been telling somebody for a while.

**Never:**
- Climb a tree to check on it
- Cut anything over a structure or a power line yourself
- Go near a tree with hanging or broken limbs in it, even on a calm day
- Touch anything within 10 feet of a power line. That's the utility's call, not yours and not mine.
